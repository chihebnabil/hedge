\title{Hedge: Tiny Learned Routing vs. Tuned Fixed Interpolation\\
in Hybrid Language Models --- A Controlled Two-Corpus Study}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{abstract}
Hybrid language models that combine neural networks with $n$-gram statistics
conventionally blend them with a small number of fixed, corpus-level weights
\cite{mikolov2011,sundermeyer2012}. We test whether replacing every fixed
weight with a \emph{learned per-token router} changes the picture in the
small-footprint regime that on-device applications occupy. \textsc{Hedge}
mixes six frozen experts---a Kneser-Ney trigram, two recency bigram experts,
a 512-token cache, a continuation unigram, and one small GRU---using an MLP
router of 2.6--13.5\,KB that reads both the experts' current votes and cheap
context evidence. Under a fixed aligned-scoring protocol on WikiText-2 and
the Penn Treebank, the learned router beats a \emph{tuned} fixed
interpolation of the \emph{same} experts at every tested seed (90.3 vs.\ 135.8
and 68.0 vs.\ 92.7 test perplexity), while static mixing fails badly
(171.0 and 101.7). Ablations locate the routing signal in the interaction
between votes and evidence, and a cost harness shows the router adds at most
8\% latency over fixed interpolation. All code, logs, and the full
reproduction pipeline are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Mixture architectures with learned routing are among the oldest ideas in
neural computation \cite{jacobs1991,jordan1994} and now scale to frontier
models \cite{shazeer2017}. We claim no architectural novelty. Instead, we ask
a question the literature has not answered at the \emph{bottom} of the
footprint range: when every expert is a frozen classical statistic plus one
small recurrent network, does learned per-token routing still beat the tuned
fixed interpolation that practitioners actually deploy?

The question matters because the fixed-weight answer is settled at larger
scales: interpolating a neural LM with Kneser-Ney smoothing using one or a
few heldout-tuned weights has been standard practice for over a decade
\cite{mikolov2011,sundermeyer2012,chang2015}. At those scales the neural
component dominates, so the blending scheme is a detail. In the
small-footprint regime---on-device, offline, CPU-only personalization
\cite{mathur2023}---the experts are comparably matched, the router is one of
the largest \emph{learnable} surface areas, and nobody has checked whether
per-token routing changes the conclusion.

We answer it with a controlled experiment. \textsc{Hedge} routes among six
experts: a modified Kneser-Ney trigram \cite{kneser1995,chen1999}, lag-2 and
lag-3 recency bigrams, a 512-token adaptive cache, a continuation unigram,
and a GRU \cite{mikolov2010,mikolov2011} of 3.3--8.2M parameters trained on
CPU. The router is a one-hidden-layer MLP of 2.6--13.5\,KB whose input pairs
the experts' \emph{votes} (their current top predictions and probabilities)
with 15 \emph{evidence} features of the context (recency, entropy, cache
state). Its hidden size is the only per-corpus hyperparameter, selected on
validation data.

\paragraph{Findings.} On two corpora (WikiText-2 \cite{merity2016} and the
Penn Treebank \cite{marcus1993,mikolov2011}) under one aligned-scoring
protocol:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item The learned router beats tuned fixed-weight interpolation of the
  same experts at \emph{every} tested seed on both corpora: 90.3 vs.\ 135.8
  (WT-2, $-33\%$) and 68.0 vs.\ 92.7 (PTB, $-27\%$).
\item Static per-corpus mixing fails (171.0 and 101.7): the win comes from
  \emph{per-token} routing, not from extra parameters.
\item The routing signal lives in the votes$\times$evidence interaction:
  gate on votes alone, 201.8; evidence alone, 367.9 (worse than equal
  mixing); both, $\sim$90--100.
\item The router costs at most 8\% extra latency over fixed interpolation
  (3.60 vs.\ 3.34\,ms/token on WT-2; on PTB it is free).
\end{itemize}

We release the full stack---every expert, the gate, the aligned evaluation
protocol, seed audits, and a one-command reproduction per corpus---to make
each number checkable.\footnote{Code, logs, and the full reproduction
pipeline are anonymized for review and will be released upon acceptance.}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Interpolated language models.} Linear interpolation of
probability estimates originates with \citet{jelinek1980} and was systematized
by Kneser-Ney smoothing \cite{kneser1995} and its empirical study
\cite{chen1999}. Our trigram expert follows this line; our interpolation
\emph{weights} do not.

\paragraph{Fixed-weight neural/$n$-gram hybrids.} RNN and LSTM LMs have long
been interpolated with Kneser-Ney models using heldout-tuned fixed weights
\cite{mikolov2011,sundermeyer2012}; \citet{chang2015} discriminatively train
a handful of context-dependent scaling factors. Our fixed-interpolation baseline
is exactly this family, and our question is whether learned per-token
routing changes their conclusion at small footprint.

\paragraph{Mixture of experts.} Gating networks date to
\citet{jacobs1991} and \citet{jordan1994}; sparse routing at scale to
\citet{shazeer2017}. That lineage learns routers for \emph{trainable}
experts, usually neural, at footprints orders of magnitude above ours. We
route among \emph{frozen} classical experts plus one small network, beneath
any footprint those systems report.

\paragraph{Prediction with expert advice.} Our hindsight oracle---the
per-position best expert---is the classic competitor bound
\cite{freund1997,cesabianchi2006}. We use it as an honest upper bound on
what any router could extract from these experts.

\section{The \textsc{Hedge} System}

\paragraph{Experts.} Six experts, all frozen after training:
(1) a modified Kneser-Ney trigram (discount scale 1.15, shared across
corpora); (2--3) lag-2 and lag-3 recency bigram experts; (4) a 512-token
adaptive cache; (5) a continuation unigram; (6) a word-level GRU
(vocabulary $+$1, 3.3M parameters on PTB, 8.2M on WT-2; Adam, cosine
schedule, early stopping on validation; batchified perplexity 86.3 on PTB).

\paragraph{Router.} A one-hidden-layer MLP with softmax over the six
experts. Its 20 input features pair each expert's \emph{vote} (top
prediction, its probability, and rank statistics) with 15 \emph{evidence}
features of the current context (cache hit rates, recency densities, token
entropy proxies). Hidden size is selected on validation from
$\{24, 64, 128, 256\}$; the selected sizes are 13.5\,KB (WT-2) and 2.6\,KB
(PTB). Training is per-position cross-entropy over collected aligned
features, with an age-invariant cache-density feature to prevent a
train/test distribution leak we detected and fixed (details in the
repository dossier).

All systems score \emph{one shared gold token stream} per corpus under a
fixed aligned protocol: exactly the positions the router sees
($\sum_s \mathrm{len}(s)-1$ per split), so every row of every table is
comparable by construction. Model selection uses validation data only. The
fixed-interpolation baseline blends KN3 and the GRU with a single
weight $\lambda$ chosen from a 201-point grid on validation
($\lambda^{*} = 0.21$ on WT-2, $0.28$ on PTB)---it receives the same tuning budget the router's hidden-size
search receives. Headline router rows are audited over three seeds and also
reported as 3-router ensembles. The oracle assigns each position its best
expert in hindsight; no realizable system can beat it.

\paragraph{The router beats tuned fixed interpolation (Table
\ref{tab:money}).} With identical experts and comparable tuning budgets,
learned per-token routing cuts perplexity by 33\% (WT-2) and 27\% (PTB)
relative to the fixed-weight baseline. The dominance is seed-robust: the \emph{worst}
router seed still beats the tuned baseline on both corpora, and both
ensembles dominate as well. Notably, fixed interpolation prefers a
\emph{more} GRU-heavy mixture on PTB ($\lambda^{*} = 0.28$ vs.\ $0.21$) yet
still loses: a single global weight cannot reweight per position.

\paragraph{Static mixing is not enough.} A learned \emph{static} mixture of
all six experts (per-corpus weights) reaches only 171.0 / 101.7 with near-zero
seed variance---the improvement comes from routing \emph{per token}, not from
added parameters.

\paragraph{Where the routing signal lives.} With the 5-expert stack on WT-2:
a gate reading votes only reaches 201.8; evidence only, 367.9 (worse than
equal mixing); both, 99.7. The evidence does not say \emph{what} to predict;
it says \emph{whose vote to trust}---the signal is the interaction.

\paragraph{Router size is corpus-dependent and validation selects correctly.}
Sweeping hidden size $\{24,64,128,256\}$: WT-2 prefers 13.5\,KB (test
144.1/92.2/\textbf{90.3}/120.5), PTB prefers 2.6\,KB (test
\textbf{68.0}/87.5/78.8/84.4). Both optima sit far below any neural
component; selection on validation picks the right one on both corpora.

\paragraph{Cost of routing.} Best-of-3 causal wall-clock over 200 test
sentences (batch-1, 4 CPU cores; energy = wall $\times$ 15\,W TDP proxy):
full system 3.60\,ms/token (WT-2) and 1.02\,ms/token (PTB) versus
3.34/1.21 for fixed interpolation --- the router's share is $\le$8\%. The GRU
dominates cost (5.09\,ms/token alone on WT-2); total footprint is 68.7\,MB
(WT-2) / 28.7\,MB (PTB).

\section{Limitations}

We report these explicitly. (1) \emph{Scale}: WT-2 and PTB are small corpora;
absolute numbers are far from modern LSTM recipes \cite{zaremba2014,
merity2017}---our claim is the controlled comparison, not state of the art.
(2) \emph{The wrinkle}: on PTB, the larger 13.5\,KB router overfits
(78.8, worse than the 5-expert gate's 72.7); the valid-selected 2.6\,KB
router and the valid-trained router do not. Router variance on the smaller
corpus is real (sd 10.1) and we report it. (3) \emph{Baselines}: we include a
toolkit-grade KenLM 5-gram \cite{heafield2011} trained with default
settings on the same streams (250.9 and 138.4 aligned test PP): it improves
on our trigram expert, as expected of a battle-tested implementation, but
sits far above every hybrid row. We do not compare against transformer
LMs---the natural stronger baseline, left to the extension. (4) One tokenizer, two English corpora, CPU
timing on one machine---all documented in the released dossier.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

In the small-footprint regime, a router of 2.6--13.5\,KB extracts 27--33\%
perplexity reduction from \emph{the same experts} that tuned fixed
interpolation blends---and static mixing cannot. We take this as evidence
that per-token routing, not parameter count, is where the small hybrid's
headroom lives. The immediate extensions of this workshop study are a
third larger corpus (WikiText-103) and a tiny transformer expert; an
extended version incorporating them is in preparation and will cite this
one.
